# On my birthday (economic and financial frameworks, currency systems, the state)

A year ago today I apparently wrote the last blog post. Same as in the Ameba Blog days: extremely slow. The rare publications continue — not that there is nothing to write, I simply do not write. Nothing on the company site either.

So today I will put down some of what I have been thinking. I thought last year’s birthday piece (that would be 9 October, tomorrow) had gone up on the birthday. It was published on the 8th. This one ↓

Much of it is the same as last year. This year, though, since coming back to Japan I have been doing a fair amount of work on climate mitigation — cutting greenhouse gases.

In that work, of course, the fact that the countries that emit a lot of greenhouse gases — or have already emitted them, in cumulative tonnes — are the rich countries after the Industrial Revolution, the so-called developed countries, comes up. Climate justice, a just transition, social justice.

I always find this odd. If you compare economic power by country or by currency, the gap in GDP per person between rich and poor countries is about two orders of magnitude. A hundredfold. At 7 percent compound, you double in ten years; cubed, that is eightfold in thirty years, roughly one order of magnitude. Twice that and you catch up in sixty years, on the arithmetic.

I think I read this somewhere: before the Industrial Revolution, the GDP-like gap in production and value between city and countryside was much the same everywhere. The Age of Exploration, then colonialism (not the colonies of ancient Greece and the like), then economic development after the Industrial Revolution — that is where all of the gap came from.

Black Lives Matter, decolonisation, collective trauma: it is all in there.

And it connects to the vaccine issue that has Guterres furious and Tedros angry as well.

Go back to the Edo period in Japan. If you try to pass a checkpoint from one domain into the next, and the neighbouring domain’s currency is one digit higher, it is hard to go (like an air ticket at ten times the price). Two digits higher and it is game-over for ordinary people. I think that is still how it feels in developing countries.

Every domain, though, has its rich: the lord, wealthy merchants, senior samurai (senior civil servants? people who have to travel for the job, or who take bribes). Those people are the exception.

If you cannot imagine this, think it through, and try to fix it, I do not see how you get as far as polar bears, the environment, climate change, or mass extinction.

This Monday, Professor Manabe won the Nobel Prize in Physics. Work from the 1960s.
ENIAC was the computer used in the Second World War.

The Dartmouth conference (artificial intelligence) was 1956.
So running calculations on a 1960s computer was fairly punishing, but physicists are good at reducing the essence to a simple equation — a model — and that is how it got done.
Fermi estimation comes from Enrico Fermi, famous in old high-school physics for beta decay, who during the war was observing an atomic-bomb test in America from a distance and estimated the yield from the strength of the wind. When you have to, you simplify the model fast.

Which brings me back. The 2030 and 2050 climate targets are fine. But we talk about the SDGs, we talk about the circular economy — shouldn’t we also sit down with the usual long-run simulations: the population models (the version that goes to 10 billion, the one that peaks earlier), how each region develops (how far China, India, ASEAN, Africa — because each of them has its own long-term goals), how they use resources, and whether it heads toward some perpetual-motion equilibrium, with a bit of technical change folded in, and actually discuss that?
(I am always pointing people at books of this kind: PwC, the *Economist* forecasts, the Club of Rome Factor 3, 4, 5 models. Almost nobody actually debates what a good future for the whole world would look like. There were Tokyo Tech scenarios for 2050 and 2100 too, and they were more “wait, what?”)

IPCC scenarios, and the climate simulations that include social scenarios of temperature change, are done in Europe, the US, and Japan. Social development is in them, so it would be interesting to discuss that. But they are climate simulations. There is no forum that takes only the social-scenario part and puts it on the table.

In my first graduate period I slid from physics into nuclear engineering. There was research as nuclear-physics calculation, but there was also a path into world resource–economy simulation (building that kind of model as part of nuclear-fuel-cycle research). So I have a soft spot for simulation.

Later, in the master’s and the doctorate: the master’s was basic research in artificial life and the like; the doctorate was a slightly crazy study of the onset of depression and anxiety in people’s work, inside society, using a social-agent simulator (artificial life, an artificial brain, the body too, so you really had to simplify, to get at the essence).

So from about August and September last year, for a few months, I told maybe fewer than ten people that mid-career Japanese leaders (people in their forties and fifties?) lacked dialogue based on a wide view, and that I would do that when I came back to Japan. It turned into a bit of a lie. (I did future-dialogue sessions around January to March instead.) If you have the time, would you spend about three months, through the end of the year, in a conversation like the one above?

That is what I found myself thinking today, talking with Kodama-san, a colleague in dialogue work.
